A brief for Westminster Theological Seminary, Learning Experience

Managing AI-Assisted Development

Borrow software engineering’s practices, not its bureaucracy. AI amplifies whatever system it enters, so the method matters more than the tool.

Written by Bern

The briefYou are the hero of this story. Where do you want to start?

The map: five chapters, built like a building with a foundation, three pillars, and a roof. Select any part to jump there.

Read a description of this picture

The picture is drawn as a building with a foundation, three pillars, and a roof. Each part is one chapter, and each rests on the one below it.

Foundation, Chapter 1: Frameworks. Published standards from software engineering supply the principles everything else rests on. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework governs the work, Google’s DORA research guides daily practice, and Agile’s values set the culture.

Red pillar, Chapter 2: Transparency and accountability. Five methods make AI-assisted work visible and owned: a written AI stance, short decision records, a time budget set before building, a spec stakeholders approve, and blameless reviews when things go wrong.

Gold pillar, Chapter 3: Documentation. At AI speed, documentation becomes part of each change rather than a chore after it. AI drafts the changelog or status note; a person curates it and chooses the right kind of document for the reader.

Teal pillar, Chapter 4: Productive friction. Research shows AI can feel faster than it is and can weaken learning. Six deliberate gates slow down only the moments that need human judgment, such as framing the problem, approving the plan, and explaining the output back before accepting it.

Roof, Chapter 5: Workflow. One eight-stage cycle draws on all three pillars at once: think before the build, work in small documented steps during it, and show and review the work after it.

The pillar colors, red, gold, and teal, mark which batch each item belongs to across the whole page.

Chapter 1: The foundation

Frameworks: what to borrow from software engineering

Five published standards supply the principles that all three practice pillars rest on.

Draws from
NIST, ISO/IEC, Google DORA, the Agile Manifesto
Feeds into
Chapters 2, 3, and 4
Mission

After this chapter you can say which framework governs the work, which guides daily practice, and why they differ in scale.

Key frameUse NIST for governance and DORA for daily practice, with Agile’s values as the culture.

The setup

Software engineering has the longest record of AI-assisted work and the most published standards. Two layers help most: a governance frame that says what must be managed, and a working frame that says how teams get good results.

Governance frame: the

The AI RMF is voluntary and built to help organizations bring trustworthiness into the design, development, use, and evaluation of AI systems (NIST 2023). It organizes the work into four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage (NIST 2024).

Its Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024) adds actions for risks that generative AI creates or makes worse (NIST 2024). For an LX team: who is responsible (Govern), what the tool touches (Map), how we test it (Measure), and what we do when it fails (Manage).

Working frame: the AI Capabilities Model

Google’s DORA program published its first report on AI-assisted development in 2025 (DORA 2025). Its central claim is that AI adoption succeeds as a systems problem, not a tools problem (DORA 2025). Of its seven capabilities, three matter most for a small LX shop (DORA n.d.):

  • A clear, communicated . Ambiguity about what AI may be used for creates risk.
  • Working in . Large AI output is hard to review; small batches keep speed from turning into instability.
  • User-centric focus. Teams without it can be harmed by adopting AI.

Secure build: and SP 800-218A

SP 800-218A extends the Secure Software Development Framework with AI-specific practices across the life cycle (Booth et al. 2024). Treat it as a checklist for anything that handles student data, not a daily workflow.

Institution: ISO/IEC 42001

The first international standard for an AI management system; it specifies how an organization establishes, maintains, and improves its governance of AI (ISO/IEC 2023). Use it to inform a WTS policy, not a one-person project.

Culture: the Agile Manifesto

People over process, working results over exhaustive paperwork, collaboration, and responding to change (Beck et al. 2001). Agile opposes only valueless documentation, not documentation itself (Nygard 2011).

Transparency, buy-in, and accountability

Read a description of this picture

Five tiles, each pairing a method with the stakeholder question it answers. The AI stance answers what AI may do here. A decision record answers why it was done this way. An appetite answers how much time the work is worth. A spec answers what exactly is being built. A blameless review answers what was learned.

2.1 Publish a project AI stance

DORA found that a clear, communicated stance on AI use lifts results; ambiguity creates risk (DORA n.d.). NIST’s Govern function asks the same: name policies, roles, and responsibilities before building (NIST 2023).

In practice: a half-page statement per project naming which AI tools were used, for what, what data they saw, and who reviewed the output.

2.2 Write

Nygard’s 2011 record has a Title, Context, Decision, Status, and Consequences, negative as well as positive (Nygard 2011). When decisions pile up accepted without understanding, people become afraid to change anything (Nygard 2011).

In practice: one record per significant choice, such as “Canvas Theme Editor JS instead of an LTI tool.” Status moves to accepted only after the stakeholder signs off.

2.3 Pitch with an

Shape Up sets a time budget before design begins, then states the problem, rough solution, risks, and no-gos (Singer 2019). Because AI makes adding features cheap, the appetite gives permission to cut scope.

2.4 Make the reviewable

Spec Kit gates AI work into specify, plan, tasks, and implement, with human review at each checkpoint (GitHub 2025). Stakeholders approve a one-page spec in plain language before any build.

2.5 Review without blame

A records impact, causes, and follow-up actions, and is a learning opportunity, not punishment (Lunney and Lueder 2016). It fixes systems, not people.

Who is accountable?

The human, always. Generative AI shifts critical thinking toward verifying information, integrating responses, and “task stewardship” (Lee et al. 2025). Name that steward on every project.

Connect to what you already know

A decision record is the rationale column of a course design map, written down at the moment of choice. An appetite works like a credit-hour workload budget: the time is fixed first, and the activities are shaped to fit it.

Chapter 3: The second pillar

Documentation, communication, and reporting

These methods keep the record current at AI speed by making documentation part of each change, not a task after it.

Draws from
DORA’s small batches, Agile’s “valuable documentation only”
Feeds into
Workflow stages 3–5 and the cadence
Mission

After this chapter you can set a documentation cadence and ask AI for the right type of document for the right reader.

Key frameDocumentation is part of the change, not a task after it: AI drafts, a human curates.

3.1 Machines draft, humans curate

A is for humans, not machines: a curated, newest-first list grouped as Added, Changed, Deprecated, Removed, Fixed, and Security (Lacan n.d.).

In practice: at the end of each AI session, ask for a draft entry in those six groups; you cut it to what a faculty member would care about.

3.3 Keep the record next to the work

Nygard kept decision records with the code they affect (Nygard 2011). Anthropic’s guidance keeps project context in a persistent file and ends each change with a descriptive commit message (Anthropic n.d.).

Without Git: one Notion page per project holds the spec, decisions, changelog, and AI stance. Paste it into each new AI session and update it before closing.

3.2 Sort documents by what the reader is doing

divides documentation into tutorials, how-to guides, reference, and explanation; mixing them produces confusing documents (Procida n.d.). Instead of “write documentation,” ask AI for one type for one reader.

3.4 Small batches make small reports

Small batches convert AI speed into stable results (DORA n.d.). A weekly note on three finished changes beats a monthly report on one sprawling build.

Chapter 4: The third pillar

Designing productive friction

These gates slow down only the moments where human judgment cannot be replaced: framing, approving, and understanding.

Draws from
NIST Measure, DORA’s small batches, three research studies
Feeds into
Workflow stages 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6
Mission

After this chapter you can name the six gates and explain why felt speed is a poor guide.

Key frameSlow down at six gates where judgment cannot be handed to AI; move freely everywhere else.
19%

Experienced developers took 19% longer on real tasks with AI allowed, yet believed afterward it had sped them up. Results may differ with other tools, projects, and experience (Becker et al. 2025).

52

Developers learning a new library with AI scored lower on a later skills quiz; engaged patterns preserved learning. A preprint: a strong signal, not settled fact (Shen and Tamkin 2026; Anthropic 2026).

319

Among 319 knowledge workers, more confidence in AI went with less critical thinking, and more self-confidence with more (Lee et al. 2025).

4.2 Six friction gates

  1. Frame the problem before prompting. Write the problem, user, and appetite yourself, without AI (Singer 2019). This protects the self-confidence linked to critical thinking (Lee et al. 2025).
  2. Run a . Imagine the project has failed and list why; it surfaces risks ordinary critique misses (Klein 2007). Ten minutes is enough.
  3. Approve the plan before any build. Explore, then plan, then code, with the human editing the plan first; letting AI jump to code can solve the wrong problem (Anthropic n.d.). Spec Kit makes this a hard gate (GitHub 2025). Ask the AI to interview you first (Anthropic n.d.).
  4. Hold the batch small. If a change can’t be described in one sentence, it needs a plan (Anthropic n.d.); if it can’t be reviewed in one sitting, split it (DORA n.d.).
  5. before accepting. Before pasting AI output into Canvas, state in your own words what it does and what could break. This is the engaged pattern that preserved skill (Shen and Tamkin 2026) and NIST’s Measure at human scale (NIST 2023).
  6. Review after launch. Hold a short blameless review after release or failure and record actions (Lunney and Lueder 2016).

4.3 Where friction does not belong

Friction everywhere becomes bureaucracy, against Agile’s preference for people over process (Beck et al. 2001). Skip the plan gate for one-sentence changes (Anthropic n.d.), and let AI move freely on drafting and formatting once the plan is approved.

Connect to what you already know

A pre-mortem is the move UDL asks of course designers: anticipate barriers before learners meet them (CAST 2024, Consideration 6.2). Explain-back is the retrieval practice you would ask of a student, turned on yourself.
